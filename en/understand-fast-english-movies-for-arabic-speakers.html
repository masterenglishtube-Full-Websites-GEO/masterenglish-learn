Why Arabic Speakers Don't Understand Fast English and Movies, and How to Fix It

Master English · أتقن الإنجليزيةUpdated 23 October 2026

You understand the subtitles, you know the words, but the actors seem to speak another language. The problem is usually not speed. In real American speech, words don't stay the way they are written.

Short answer. Native speakers change words in four predictable ways: the T disappears or turns into a soft D, unstressed syllables shrink to a weak “uh” (schwa), words link into each other, and common phrases are shortened (going to → gonna). Learn the four changes, then train with short movie scenes in three stages.

The four sound changes

ChangeWrittenWhat you actually hear
Vanishing T (after N)internet, center, interview, twentyinnernet, cener, innerview, twenny
Flap T (between vowels)water, better, put it awaywa-der, bedder, pud-id-away
Schwa (weak “uh”)tomato, today, police, rock and rolltuh-may-duh, tuh-day, puh-lease, rock n' roll
Linkinghold on, go away, pick it uphol-don, go-(w)-away, pikitup
Reductionsgoing to, what are you going to do?gonna, whaddaya gonna do?

Why it is harder for Arabic speakers

Arabic has no separate schwa sound, so Arabic speakers tend to pronounce every syllable fully and then expect to hear every syllable. The flap T is also unfamiliar to an ear trained on a clear Arabic ت. Once you know what to listen for, the same scenes start to make sense.

A practice method that works

  1. Understand: watch a short scene (one minute) with Arabic subtitles, so you know what is happening.
  2. Hunt the language: watch it again with English subtitles and note the phrases that sound different from how they are written.
  3. Shadow the actor: play one sentence at a time and say it with the same rhythm and linking, until you can say it with them.

Ten minutes a day on one scene beats an hour of passive watching.

FAQ

Why can't I understand English in movies even though I know the words?

Because spoken American English changes words: the T disappears or becomes a soft D, unstressed syllables shrink to a schwa, words link together, and phrases are shortened (gonna, wanna). Once you learn these four changes, you can recognize the words you already know.

Is the problem that native speakers talk too fast?

Usually not. The bigger problem is that words don't sound the way they are written. Learning the sound changes helps more than slowing the video down.

How should I practice listening with movies?

Take one short scene: watch it with Arabic subtitles to understand it, then with English subtitles to spot the changed phrases, then shadow the actor sentence by sentence.
